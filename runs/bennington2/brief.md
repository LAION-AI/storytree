# Brief — the second pass at Bennington. Bigger, dirtier, four plots.

The first version was praised for structure and criticised for being *impeccable*:
everybody behaved well, nobody interrupted, nobody was unfair, and forty minutes
of decent people being restrained at each other is the scene that dies in the
read-through. This one is built against that.

## The frame, unchanged

A closed virtual-reality match rendered as a fog-bound American town in 1984.
Players — **guests** — enter as vampires, cannot log out, and must feed on the
town's inhabitants — **hosts** — who are artificial minds that believe
themselves real. Sleep erases a host's memory of the waking hours. The only
exits are elimination or a dawn ritual that ends the match for whoever completes
it, and pays out once.

Analog horror crossed with neon-noir: arcade cabinets, cassettes, CRT glow, a
synth score, a college town with money in it.

## What is different this time

**Scale.** At least **four plots**, ideally five, genuinely interleaved — not
four threads running in parallel but four that keep spending each other's
resources. Longer: aim at feature length rather than a single episode.

**Money is a plot, not a detail.** Somebody is running the economy of this
match: tribute, protection, debt, a market in access to hosts. Blood is a
currency and currencies get cornered. Somebody is being extorted and somebody is
skimming. Make the ledger legible enough that an audience can follow who owes
whom.

**Intrigue.** Guests lie to each other, form alliances of convenience, and
betray them for reasons an audience can reconstruct afterwards. At least one
reversal should depend on information the audience had and misread. Nobody is
purely a schemer; everyone is protecting something.

**Tech and analysis are on screen.** The protagonist reads the world as a
system, and we watch him do it — measuring, timing, testing a rule to
destruction. But he is not the only competent one: someone else is better than
him at something that matters, and it costs him.

**Philosophy of mind, as situation only.** Whether the hosts are conscious is
never debated aloud. It is decided by what people *do* — who is protected, who
is spent, who apologises to whom and whether the apology is heard. If any
character delivers a speech about consciousness, the scene is wrong. The
question must arrive as a practical problem: whose word counts, who can consent,
what a promise to a mind that will forget it is worth.

**Sex, drugs and rock and roll — as pressure, not as decoration.** There is a
party scene and it is not innocent. There is a band, or a bar with a stage, and
the music is diegetic and specific. There are substances, and they change what
people are capable of and what they can be held to. There is desire, and it
cuts across the strategic lines in ways that cost people their plans.

Handle the intimacy the way a good adult screenplay does: **strongly present,
never explicit on the page.** What we see is the approach, the aftermath, the
consequence and the cost. The scene cuts before the act and picks up after it.
Explicitness would be less powerful and is not what this is for. The same
discipline applies to violence: it lands, it has weight, and it is not
choreographed for pleasure.

**Somebody must behave badly.** Repeatedly. Not villainously — *badly*. A cheap
shot at exactly the wrong moment. An interruption that costs someone their one
chance to say a thing. A cruelty done out of tiredness rather than malice. A
promise broken because keeping it had become inconvenient. At least a third of
the scenes must contain a moment their character would be ashamed of on
reflection.

**Everyone must sound different.** Give each speaking character a voice that
survives the name being deleted from the page: sentence shape, what they reach
for metaphors from, what they will not say, how they change under pressure. Two
characters whose lines could be swapped are one character wearing two names.

## What must still be true

- The world's rules are hard, consistent, and cost something. Nobody improvises
  a new power.
- At least two hosts become full characters with their own wants, not devices
  for a guest's education.
- The rivals from the first version remain available — the psychologist, the
  architect, the syndicate boss, the hedonist, the hunter — but at least two of
  them come on screen this time and get their own logic.
- The ending is not a victory and not a condemnation. Several people choose, and
  the costs are not distributed fairly.
- Target audience: educated adults who read for tech, philosophy, sex, romance
  and action, and who will notice if the philosophy is decoration.
