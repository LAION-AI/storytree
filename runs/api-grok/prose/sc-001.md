INT. THE FORGE-HOUSE - DAY

Morning through a high slit. Heat holds at the hearth and fails two steps toward the door. Scale. Wet wool. A short stack of unfinished bars takes up the walking space by the anvil.

WILLA, compact, cloth singed at the edge, sets a bar on the pan of the old beam-balance. She waits. The arm hunts, then steadies.

ORREN stands at the counterweight. Stooped, thin, the day's fire already drawn behind him. He does not look at the door. She does not either.

                    WILLA
          Short.

                    ORREN
          Leave it on.

She adds a second bar. The arm drops. She lets it hunt.

                    WILLA
          Pair.

                    ORREN
          I have the heat. Read it.

                    WILLA
          I am reading it.

The arm settles. She leans to the marks on the beam.

                    WILLA
          Four and a quarter under the stamp.

                    ORREN
          On the first?

                    WILLA
          On the pair. First bar two under. Second two and a quarter.

He slides the counterweight. The arm lifts, hunts, comes level.

                    ORREN
          Four and a quarter.

                    WILLA
          I have it.

                    ORREN
          I am not arguing the arm.

                    WILLA
          I did not ask you to.

She takes the slate off the bench nail. Chalk. She writes the figure. He watches the arm, not the slate.

                    WILLA
          Pair three. Four and a quarter light. Upper cut.

                    ORREN
          Upper cut was always light.

                    WILLA
          Last year a pair ran two under. This is worse.

                    ORREN
          Fuel was worse. You cannot draw what the fire will not hold.

                    WILLA
          I am entering four and a quarter. Not a fault in your heat.

                    ORREN
          Put it down as it sits.

                    WILLA
          It is down.

They leave the bars on the pan. The arm holds. Neither lifts the iron.

                    ORREN
          How many pairs left?

                    WILLA
          Three. Then the offcuts. Offcuts will not make a bar.

                    ORREN
          Weigh the three. Offcuts stay for mends.

                    WILLA
          If I throw the offcuts in we still come short.

                    ORREN
          I know what we come.

Wind at the stuck door. He keeps one hand on the counterweight and stands nearer the fire than the work needs.

                    WILLA
          Next pair.

She lifts the first pair off, sets the next on. The arm drops harder.

                    WILLA
          Worse. Five under.

                    ORREN
          Both?

                    WILLA
          The left is three. The right is two. Five.

He moves the weight. Waits. Nods at the arm.

                    ORREN
          Five. Write five.

                    WILLA
          I am writing five.

She chalks it. Leaves this pair on the pan as well, long enough that the arm has to hold the number.

                    WILLA
          Twenty-one days.

                    ORREN
          I can count.

                    WILLA
          I am putting the days on the slate with the weight. So the pile has a date on it.

                    ORREN
          Midwinter takes this iron. It takes the village weight with it. Both. Same morning. Together.

                    WILLA
          I know the compact.

                    ORREN
          Highkettle has no second piece. No coin for one. No grain put by if they shut the pass.

                    WILLA
          I have the count.

                    ORREN
          I am not asking if you have the count.

                    WILLA
          Then you are telling me work I already have in front of me.

                    ORREN
          I am telling you there is no reserve. So the short bars still go. The relic still goes. We do not get another winter to make them right.

                    WILLA
          The bars are honest. They are only thin.

                    ORREN
          Honest and thin is still thin when they set them on a table.

                    WILLA
          Will they weigh them?

                    ORREN
          They count bars. They do not always weigh. We do not stand there and hope they forget how numbers work.

She chalks another mark. Still does not look at the door, toward the path down to the hearths.

                    WILLA
          Grain-right holds if we present. Pass stays open. I have that.

                    ORREN
          Then keep it as work. Not as a question.

                    WILLA
          It was not a question.

                    ORREN
          Good.

She sets the chalk down. Looks at the arm. Looks at him. A space where another sentence could go.

                    WILLA
          The board this year—

She stops. Picks the chalk up. Sets it down again.

                    ORREN
          Next heat is yours.

                    WILLA
          The stock is still on the pan.

                    ORREN
          I said the next heat is yours. Draw it thin. Do not chase mass you have not got in the bloom.

                    WILLA
          I know how to draw a thin bar.

                    ORREN
          Then do it. I will take the bellows.

                    WILLA
          You take the bellows when the count is short.

                    ORREN
          The draft is bad.

                    WILLA
          The draft is what it is.

He does not take that up. She waits. She does not finish the sentence about the board.

                    WILLA
          How many in the next heat?

                    ORREN
          Two. If the fire holds. If it does not, one, and we eat the day.

                    WILLA
          Soup is already thin.

                    ORREN
          Then do not waste charcoal on a third.

                    WILLA
          Last of the good sack is half. The wet sack will spit.

                    ORREN
          Use the good. Wet coal makes a dirty weld and we cannot show a dirty weld.

                    WILLA
          We cannot show a short stack either.

                    ORREN
          We show what we have. Lift them.

                    WILLA
          I can stay later.

                    ORREN
          You always stay later.

                    WILLA
          That is not what I was saying.

                    ORREN
          I know what you were not saying. Lift them.

She lifts the bars off the pan. The empty arm swings up. He takes one from her because the pair is awkward, not because she cannot carry it.

They set the bars back on the unfinished pile. The pile is short. Anyone in the room can see it.

                    ORREN
          Slate.

                    WILLA
          I wrote it. Pair three, four and a quarter. Pair four, five. Days, twenty-one.

                    ORREN
          Then the heat.

She goes to the hearth. He works the bellows. The fire comes up thin and clean. Neither of them speaks toward the village, or the chest, or the years of board.

                    WILLA
          Ready.

                    ORREN
          Take it. Two bars. No more.

                    WILLA
          Two.

She seats the stock. He keeps the bellows. The house is only the fire and the short pile and the slate with the true weight on it.
