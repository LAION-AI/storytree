INT. AL'S DEPOT DINER - NIGHT

1:40. Fog on the front glass. The neon clock hums. The radio is low. Six empty booths.

HALE comes in off the street. He does not take a stool. He walks the counter to the far end, kitchen side, and stops there.

CORA watches his hands, then the door.

                    CORA
          Stool's free.

                    HALE
          I'm not eating.

                    CORA
          Then you're in the way of the
          ones who are.

                    HALE
          There aren't any.

She sets a cup on the Formica. Pours. The pot is almost gone.

                    CORA
          Dollar ten if you drink it.
          Dollar ten if you don't. Till
          doesn't care.

                    HALE
          Keep it.

                    CORA
          I don't keep what you don't
          order.

She slides the cup an inch. He does not touch it. He checks the exit, then her hands.

                    CORA
          That's three nights in the
          coat. I put a face with a
          coat. I put a coat with a
          check. You don't give me
          either.

                    HALE
          I don't need a check.

                    CORA
          I need a name for one.

                    HALE
          There isn't one.

                    CORA
          There is if you stand at my
          end. First name. Town. You
          want coffee, you say coffee.
          You want the alley, it's
          behind the brick. What I
          don't do is hold a pot while
          a man counts my door.

                    HALE
          Please.

The word lands like a coin. She does not pick it up.

                    CORA
          Please what.

                    HALE
          The cup's fine where it is.

                    CORA
          Then why are you at this
          end.

He takes her wrist. Not hard. The cup stays put.

Her shoulders go before her face does. She locks her knees. He bends to the inside of the wrist and holds there, as if a mark he already measured were written on the skin.

The neon clock keeps its hum. The radio finds the end of a song and does not start another.

                    CORA
          That's the working hand.
          You put it down. You put it
          down or this till closes and
          you stand on the sidewalk
          with the rest of the fog.

He stops while she is still upright. Color has gone out of her mouth. She sets the free hand on the counter. The knees hold.

He lays the wrist back on the Formica the way he would a finished check.

                    HALE
          Thank you.

                    CORA
          That is not how a till
          works.

                    HALE
          I know how a till works.

                    CORA
          Then you put a single in the
          can and you don't come back
          to this end without a plate.

He is already looking at the door. He does not rush it. He leaves her standing. The cup is still full. The street takes him.

She stays at the empty end until the knees are hers. She dumps the cup. She counts the tip can twice. She lifts the Polaroid off her chest, carries it through the kitchen, and puts it in the break-room locker. She shuts the door.

INT. AL'S DEPOT DINER - BREAK ROOM - MORNING

A cot. A mute TV on a test card. One closed locker. No one else.

Cora surfaces. She sits up too fast. She waits. The room settles.

She looks at the wrist. She looks at the door to the diner.

                    CORA
          Left end. His hand. Clock
          said three.

She stands. In the doorway the neon clock is still lit against the day. It does not say three.

                    CORA
          You don't say three.

She opens the locker. The camera is where she put it. Under it, a sheaf.

Top print: the alley behind the diner. Wet brick. A window lit that she does not light. No pencil on the back.

                    CORA
          I don't shoot the alley. I
          don't close at that hour.

Under it, another empty room she cannot place. Under that: the man in the charcoal coat, a different night, not looking at the lens.

She counts four she has no date for. She opens the pack in the camera and counts what is left.

                    CORA
          Four gone. I didn't pull
          four.

She takes the sheaf to the prep table. She lines them. Alley. The room. The coat. A fourth with no hour she will own.

She does not go to the time clock.

The TV crawls. A seam of roll-bar climbs the test card and holds, a black stroke that will not pass.

She looks at it once.

                    CORA
          Set's broken.

She looks back at the prints. She does not clock in.
