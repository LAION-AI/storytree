EXT. MAIN STREET - NIGHT

Thin neon on brick. At both ends of the street the fog smears like a bad tape.

HALE walks the sidewalk, overcoat neat, a folded paper map in one hand, a pencil in the other. He checks doors, then alley mouths, then windows. Faces last.

A DRUGGIST turns a key in a glass door. His collar is open.

                    DRUGGIST
          You need something? I just locked it.

                    HALE
          What time do you open.

                    DRUGGIST
          Nine. Same as always. You look like you
          want a bottle.

                    HALE
          Nine is fine.

                    DRUGGIST
          You passing through? Because if you are,
          the road's been dumping people back all
          week. I keep seeing the same taillights.

Hale writes on the map. He does not look at the open collar again.

                    HALE
          Thank you.

                    DRUGGIST
          I'm not a tourist desk. You want aspirin,
          you come back at nine.

Hale is already at the next stoop. A service door sits unlatched. He marks it. He does not go in.

INT. BRIGGS AND SON - NIGHT

Fluorescents. Paint in ranks. TOM BRIGGS counts the drawer without looking up.

                    BRIGGS
          We're closed. Sign says so.

                    HALE
          A flashlight.

                    BRIGGS
          Aisle two, with the batteries. You want D
          or C. Don't say both. I already counted.

                    HALE
          D. Please.

Hale brings the light and a blister pack to the counter. Briggs writes the invoice in a heavy hand. The header reads BRIGGS.

                    BRIGGS
          Eight ninety-nine. You want a bag, that's
          extra and it's paper and it rips.

                    HALE
          No bag.

Hale lays exact bills on the wood. He does not add a tip. He puts the leftover single back in the clip.

                    BRIGGS
          You new, or lost, or just the kind that
          shops at eleven.

                    HALE
          Just the light.

                    BRIGGS
          Nobody's just the light. I was going to
          take the truck up to the lake this
          weekend. Fish don't care what day it is.
          Then I thought about the stairs, and the
          stock, and—

He stops. He does not finish. He slides the flashlight across.

                    BRIGGS
          Side door if you remember something.
          I live over. Don't bang if it's paint.
          Paint waits.

                    HALE
          Thank you.

Hale does not go to the door. He walks back toward the paint. Briggs comes down the aisle with the keys.

                    BRIGGS
          I said aisle two. Store's done. Whatever
          you forgot, it forgot you back.

Hale looks at him. Briggs's mouth stays open around the next line and never finds it.

Hale takes him at the collar, close and quiet. Briggs's knees go. The color leaves his face. Hale stops while Briggs is still on his feet and sets him against the paint shelf until Briggs's own hand finds a can.

A mark sits at the collar.

                    BRIGGS
          I still got to lock up.

                    HALE
          Sit down.

                    BRIGGS
          It's the stairs. Always the stairs. I
          already wrote you up. You paid.

He tries to count the keys. Loses the number. Starts over.

                    BRIGGS
          Did you pay.

                    HALE
          Yes.

                    BRIGGS
          Then I don't owe you a bag.

Hale does not rush the front door.

INT. BENNINGTON MEMORIAL - NIGHT

A cold corridor. A vending machine hums. DENISE COLE writes at a charting station. Badge: COLE, D.

Hale stands at the station as if he has a reason.

                    DENISE
          Visiting hours ended at eight. If you
          want coffee, the machines are by the
          elevator. This is medical.

                    HALE
          Coffee. Thank you.

                    DENISE
          That is not a thing I do. If you're here
          for Lang, he coded at six. Family's in
          the lobby. If you're not here for Lang,
          you're not here.

                    HALE
          I'm not here for Lang.

                    DENISE
          Then you can walk yourself downstairs.
          I'm not getting pulled off this floor
          because someone wandered in looking for
          a machine. I have a midnight pulse check
          and a chart that already doesn't match
          the bed.

                    HALE
          I'll wait.

                    DENISE
          You will not. I can call security, or
          you can drink whatever you buy off this
          floor. Those are the choices. Sir.

She looks up to finish it. He is already close. The pen stops. Her voice stays even.

He takes her the same way, measured, at the throat above the cardigan. She stays in the chair. He stops while she can still sit. A bruise darkens above the collar.

He feeds the vending machine coins, comes back, sets the paper cup at her elbow.

                    DENISE
          That's not from the cafeteria.

                    HALE
          No.

                    DENISE
          Midnight. I said midnight. I still have
          a pulse to count.

She looks at the coffee. She does not look at him. Her hand finds the bruise and comes away as if it were a smudge on the chart.

                    HALE
          Thank you.

He leaves. She sits. The cup steams. She watches the clock, then the chart, then not the door.

INT. AL'S DEPOT DINER - NIGHT

Graveyard empty. A radio works through a song. Over the pie case a CRT rolls a bar through a silent picture. A cassette deck sits by the radio, unlatched, no tape in it.

CORA VOSS pours into a clean mug she has not been asked for. Faded green dress. Nametag: CORA, paint chipped. A Polaroid hangs on a strap across her chest.

Hale sits at the counter. Kitchen door first. Her hands. The register. Then her face.

                    CORA
          Menu or you know.

                    HALE
          Whatever you're still cooking.

                    CORA
          Meatloaf. It's leftover and it's honest.
          Coffee's real. Pie isn't. I wouldn't.

                    HALE
          Please.

She sets the plate. Pours. She does not linger. She counts a coffee can by the register twice and does not take a bill from it.

                    CORA
          You're not a regular.

                    HALE
          No.

                    CORA
          That's fine. Regulars talk. You just
          have to pay. Last bus is gone if you
          were hoping. Next one's a story people
          tell.

Hale eats. He finishes the plate.

                    CORA
          Four sixty. You want anything else, say
          it now. I don't like a man sitting on a
          clean plate.

Hale sets exact bills on the check. Then a folded extra, more than four sixty. He stands and puts the extra in the coffee can himself.

                    CORA
          That's more than the check.

                    HALE
          I know.

                    CORA
          I don't make change out of the can. The
          can isn't the till.

                    HALE
          I don't need change.

                    CORA
          Then you're paid. You can go. I still
          have a close, and I don't need a witness
          for the empty rooms.

She reaches for the plate.

He takes her wrist. Same economy. No fuss. Color leaves her face. She locks her knees and stays standing. He stops.

The radio keeps the song.

                    CORA
          You're paid up. The plate's done. Four
          sixty, and the can is not a conversation.

                    HALE
          Thank you.

She puts the plate in the bin without dropping it. She does not sit. She pours coffee she does not drink. Her shoulders have already flinched. Her face is late.

                    CORA
          Door's that way. I lock it when I lock
          it.

He does not fill the silence. He leaves the clean plate and the open door.

EXT. AL'S DEPOT DINER - NIGHT

Register light on the sidewalk. Hale unfolds the map against the glass.

He writes a window of hours. His hand stops.

He writes BRIGGS. Then COLE.

The hand stops again.

He writes CORA.

Three names. Three times. He folds the map. He does not look back through the glass, where she is still standing at the counter, still precise, Polaroid against her ribs.
