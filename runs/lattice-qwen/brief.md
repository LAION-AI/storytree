# THE LATTICE — story brief

## Logline

A quality-assurance auditor whose job is finding rendering faults in a municipal
simulation discovers that the faults are messages, that the city she audits is a
containment vessel for eleven thousand uploaded minds, and that the people paying
her to fix the glitches are the ones being smuggled out.

## Form and audience

Feature screenplay. Roughly 70% dialogue, 30% action. Adult audience that reads
science fiction for ideas as well as tension: philosophy of mind, information
security, institutional rot, and the ethics of consent for people who cannot give
it. Hard-edged, not bleak. Intelligence is the pleasure on offer.

## Setting

**Vantage Municipal** — a mid-sized city, permanently overcast, that is not a
place. It is a simulation run by a public-private consortium called the Ledger
Authority. Inside it live eleven thousand people who died between 2029 and 2044
and whose neural scans were purchased, in bulk, from hospital estates. None of
them knows this. They believe it is 2051 and that they live in a coastal city
with an unusually good transit system.

Vantage runs at nine-tenths real time to save compute. The residents cannot
perceive the difference. Outside, in the physical world, the Authority sells two
products: **tenancy** (a scanned mind placed inside Vantage, sold to the dying as
an afterlife) and **extraction** (access to what those minds still know — trade
secrets, passwords, buried testimony — sold quietly to whoever pays).

### In-world mechanics that must stay consistent

- **Rendering faults** — Vantage is compressed. Objects nobody is looking at are
  stored as descriptions rather than as things. A fault is what happens when the
  description is restored wrong: a door that opens onto the wrong room, rain that
  falls upward for a second, a colleague's face that is briefly a stranger's.
  Residents forget faults within about ninety seconds. This forgetting is enforced
  by the substrate, not by psychology.
- **Auditors** — residents given a partial exemption from the forgetting so they
  can report faults. They are told they work for the city. They are the only
  people inside who can hold a contradiction in mind long enough to reason about
  it. There are nine of them.
- **The Ledger** — the record of every mind in Vantage and what it is worth.
  Tenancy is a cost line; extraction is a revenue line. A mind whose extraction
  value has been fully realised is a pure cost, and the Authority's quarterly
  obligation is to reduce costs.
- **Redaction** — deleting a resident. Inside Vantage it presents as an ordinary
  death, and everyone who knew them keeps the memories. The Authority considers
  this humane and says so in writing.
- **Carriage** — smuggling a mind out of Vantage into a physical substrate. It
  takes roughly forty minutes of sustained bandwidth, it is visible on the
  Authority's own monitoring as a rendering fault of unusual size, and it can only
  be run from inside.

## Protagonist

**IMOGEN SARR**, 41, senior auditor. Precise, unsentimental, extremely good at her
job, and proud of that in a way she would not admit. She has audited Vantage for
nine subjective years. She believes she is a civil servant in a functioning city.

Her defining internal conflict is not "is this real" — she gets there early and it
is not the interesting question. It is this: **she is very good at a job whose
actual function is to make a prison run smoothly.** Every fault she has closed was
a message someone was trying to send. Competence has been her identity and her
self-respect, and competence turns out to have been the mechanism. She cannot
disown the work without disowning herself, and she cannot continue it once she
knows. That tension must drive her scenes, not exposition about simulation theory.

She also has a specific, concrete want that is not abstract: her sister **NOOR**
is in Vantage, was scanned three years after her, and does not know either. Noor's
extraction value was realised eleven months ago.

## Other principals

- **ELIAS KETT**, 60s, Imogen's supervisor inside Vantage. A resident himself,
  though he learned it decades ago and made his peace by deciding the residents
  are better off. Genuinely kind, genuinely doing harm, and the most persuasive
  voice for the Authority's position. He must never be a hypocrite — he must be
  right often enough to be dangerous.
- **DELPHINE ORR**, 30s, outside. An Authority compliance officer who authorises
  redactions and has begun falsifying them — recording a mind as deleted while
  actually carrying it out. She is not a hero: she is doing it for money at first,
  and the ethics arrive late and inconveniently.
- **THE PORTER**, unknown, a voice that reaches Imogen through the faults. Wants
  her to run a carriage. Refuses to explain why her, which is itself the tell.
- **NOOR SARR**, 36, Imogen's sister. Not a prize to be rescued. She has her own
  read on what is happening and, when told, does not want what Imogen wants.

## Plots — at least four, interlocking

1. **The audit** — Imogen tracks a pattern in the faults, discovers the messages,
   and learns what Vantage is. External spine, drives the first act.
2. **The carriage** — the operation to smuggle a mind out. Physical, timed,
   procedural, and it requires Imogen to use every professional skill she now
   despises. Provides the set pieces.
3. **The ledger** — Delphine outside, discovering how redaction decisions are
   actually made and what quarterly cost reduction means in practice. Institutional
   horror, mostly in offices, mostly in dialogue.
4. **The sisters** — Imogen and Noor. Carriage bandwidth exists for one mind.
   Noor does not want to go. This is the plot the others are in service of.
5. **Kett's argument** — an ongoing philosophical dispute between Imogen and Elias
   about whether an unknowing life is a life. It must be a genuine argument in
   which he sometimes wins, and it must resolve in action rather than in a speech.

## Direction — how to write it

- Show through behaviour and conflict. No character explains the world to another
  character who already knows it.
- Every scene carries both an internal and an external conflict. A scene that has
  only one is a scene to cut.
- Rendering faults are used sparingly and always mean something. They are not
  set dressing and never a spectacle for its own sake.
- The philosophy arrives inside arguments people are having for personal reasons.
  Nobody monologues about the nature of reality.
- Dialogue is specific to speaker. If two characters could swap lines without
  anyone noticing, the scene is broken.
- The Authority's people speak in the language of cost, obligation and duty of
  care. They are never cackling. The banality is the horror.
- Earn the ending. The final choice must cost Imogen something she has spent the
  whole film establishing that she wants.

## Plot embedding — intended coordinates

Primary genres: science fiction, thriller, drama. Secondary: political/institutional
critique, family drama. Deliberately low on: comedy, romance as a main line,
spectacle-driven action.

High on: interiority, moral ambiguity, institutional power, intellectual content,
dramatic irony, betrayal, identity destabilisation.
Moderate: violence, pace, spectacle.
Low: humour, sentimentality, romance, wish-fulfilment.

## Scale

Feature length. Aim for roughly 45–60 scenes across 5 plots, 30–40 entities.
