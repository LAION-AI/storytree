02What speculative decoding actually does

Generating text one token at a time is bandwidth-bound, not compute-bound. To produce a single token the GPU must stream the active weights through memory — here 18.4 GB per token. The arithmetic itself is trivial; the reading is what costs. The card spends its time waiting on memory with most of its compute idle.

Speculative decoding exploits that idle compute. Something cheap guesses the next few tokens, and then the big model checks all the guesses in a single pass — because verifying four tokens costs almost exactly what generating one costs, given that the weight read is the same either way.

without read 18.4 GB → 1 token read 18.4 GB → 1 token read 18.4 GB → 1 token three reads, three tokens with guess: ["scene", "_id", "\": \"", "sc-"] read 18.4 GB → verify all four at once → 3 accepted, 1 rejected one read, three tokens

Guesses that are right are free. Guesses that are wrong cost nothing but the discarded guess. So the speedup is set by the acceptance rate — how often the cheap guesser is right.

That was the theory, and it predicted this workload would be the ideal case: our output is schema-bound JSON with the same keys over and over, about the most predictable token stream there is. Acceptance rates rise as entropy falls, so structured output should sit at the top of the usual 1.5–2× band or above.

Measured — and the prediction was wrong
Speculative decoding made this model slower. Not marginally: 25% slower than doing nothing at all.

Same 50k prompt, greedy decode, prompt cache pre-warmed so only decode is compared, 2500 tokens each:

Why the intuition fails, and it is the architecture

The claim that verification is nearly free holds on a dense model, where the same weights are read no matter which tokens you are checking. GLM-5.2 is a sparse mixture of experts: 8 of 256 experts fire per token, chosen per token. Each extra position in the verification batch therefore pulls in its own set of 8 routed experts, so the union of weights that must be streamed grows roughly linearly with draft length. Verification is not free — it is nearly as expensive as generation.

At 21% acceptance you pay for about 4.7 drafted tokens to keep one, and those extra expert gathers cost more than the tokens saved. On a 40 B-active MoE the break-even acceptance rate is somewhere around 55–65%, not the ~30% that would suffice on a dense model. Low entropy was not enough to get an n-gram drafter there.

This is the useful transferable finding, and it cuts against the general advice: on sparse MoE models, speculative decoding needs a far better drafter than the folklore suggests, and a mediocre one is actively harmful.

Two routes are open, and one is already paid for

The build supports:

draft-mtpdraft-eagle3 draft-dsparkdraft-dflash draft-simple ngram-cachengram-mod ngram-simplengram-map-k

MTP — the draft weights are already in the file. GLM-5.2 ships a multi-token-prediction head as layer blk.78. The loader currently discards it: 26 tensors, 4.56 GiB, each logged as "unused tensor … ignoring". That is a purpose-built guesser, already downloaded, already sitting on disk, doing nothing. An earlier read of this said MTP needed a sidecar the repo does not publish; the loader output contradicts that. Reading the server source settles it: the speculative init builds the draft context from the target model already resident in memory — no second model, no -md flag. The tensors are dropped only because nothing asks for them.

So --spec-type draft-mtp was a one-flag experiment costing about 4.56 GiB against 250 GiB free — and it had to clear roughly 55–65% acceptance just to break even.

Measured — and this one wins decisively
draft-mtp: 24.26 tok/s against 16.70 baseline. +45%, at 79.6% acceptance.

The trained head clears the break-even bar with room to spare — 3.39 tokens accepted per round against the ~1 needed to break even. And because speculative decoding is exactly-equivalent by construction, the 45% costs nothing in quality. It is the rare free lunch.

03bConcurrency: the measurement that changed the plan

Eight cards, one model, layer-split. The obvious hope is that running many requests at once recovers the idle seven. This is the question the whole 8-GPU case rests on, so it was measured rather than assumed — -np 8, MTP enabled, 1500 tokens per stream:

It does not scale at all
Aggregate throughput is flat at ~26–27 tok/s across an eightfold range. Per stream it divides exactly by N.

Eight concurrent calls finish in the time eight sequential calls would take. The box has a hard ceiling around 27 tok/s and no amount of restructuring lifts it.

The mechanism is the same one that sank ngram-cache, which is what makes it worth stating as a rule. Different sequences route to different experts, so batching N sequences needs up to 8N distinct expert weight reads per step. Traffic scales with N; there is nothing to amortise. Dense models batch beautifully. A 256-expert top-8 MoE at small batch does not.

The unifying rule

On a sparse MoE at small batch, anything that widens the batch — speculative verification or concurrent sequences — pays close to full weight-traffic cost per extra token. Only a drafter accurate enough to clear roughly 65% acceptance beats that. It is why MTP wins at 79.6% and why both ngram-cache at 21.3% and plain concurrency lose. One sentence covers three separate measurements, two of which contradicted a confident prediction.

Practical consequence: do not restructure a long job from serial to parallel. Issue calls sequentially and spend the effort on prompt-cache prefix stability instead.

The n-gram variants need no draft model at all. They guess from repetition in the text so far — which is exactly what a document full of repeated JSON keys provides. Zero extra VRAM, zero extra download.

The named-architecture drafters — eagle3, dspark, dflash — each want a separately trained draft model matched to the base. For an abliterated GLM-5.2 those do not exist, so they are compatible in the sense of being present in the build, and unusable in the sense of having nothing to load.

04Would two endpoints of four GPUs be faster?

This is the question worth answering carefully, because the intuition — twice the endpoints, twice the throughput — is right about concurrency and wrong about everything else.

First, the good quant does not fit on four

Measured live footprint at 128k context: 380.2 GiB. Four A100s give 314.9 GiB usable. Even with the KV cache stripped to nothing the weights alone leave under 0.1 GiB of margin. This is not a close call — the minimum for UD-Q3_K_M is five GPUs. And there is no Q4 in the abliterated repo at all; Q3_K_M is the largest thing published.

Second, the honest case for 2×4

It is not nothing. A smaller quant reads fewer bytes per token, so each request also decodes faster — UD-Q2_K_MXFP4 has a roofline of 150 tok/s against Q3's 111, roughly 36% more headroom. Two independent endpoints then double concurrency on top of that. For a batch of independent calls, 2×4 at Q2 could plausibly beat 1×8 at Q3 on aggregate tokens per second.

Recommendation
One 8-GPU endpoint at Q3_K_M, not two 4-GPU endpoints at Q2.

Two smaller endpoints would double throughput on the axis we are least short of — concurrency — while degrading the axis this pipeline actually depends on: getting one long structured call right the first time.

Three specifics make sub-3-bpw a bad trade for this workload, as opposed to in general:

  • Long structured emissions. Our real calls run to 29,190 output tokens (entities) and 27,563 (events). Low-bit quantisation degrades exactly the narrow-margin decisions — a brace, a quote, a consistent key name deep in a nested structure — and one bad token invalidates the call. We already need repair passes at full precision: three of eight calls in the last run. The repair rate is what would get worse, and repairs are themselves 30–45k-token calls.
  • Long-range state precision, and it fails silently. The patch design means a scene at t=25 is only correct if an attribute was tracked correctly across 24 prior patches. Low-bit quantisation erodes precisely that, and it produces well-formed JSON with a wrong value — which no schema validator catches.
  • Abliteration has already spent part of the budget. These weights are refusal-ablated and quantised. Stacking 2.2 bpw on top compounds two independent sources of degradation.

If concurrency is what we need, it comes from --parallel N slots on the one 8-GPU endpoint. Batched decode on a MoE this sparse scales well, because extra sequences reuse the same expert weight reads — the expensive part is amortised across the batch.

Fair caveat: the quality judgement above is engineering reasoning, not measurement. Measuring it properly would mean downloading a second 253 GB quant and running a long-range consistency eval. That was not done.

The largest lever, by a distance
About 74% of every token this model generates for us is hidden reasoning. Suppressing it takes a full story from ~8.2 h to ~2.6 h.

GLM-5.2's chat template defaults to Reasoning Effort: Max. Left alone, three quarters of the wall-clock goes to tokens nobody reads. That 3.2× dwarfs anything speculative decoding was ever going to deliver — the boring knob beat the interesting one.

There is a reason to think this is unusually safe here, though it needs measuring rather than assuming. The scaffold already forces the reasoning to be written out explicitly into the schema — trajectories, theory of mind to three degrees, rejected alternatives, the specimen exchange. The model is being made to think on the page. Hidden thinking on top may be largely duplicated work, in which case switching it off is close to free. If instead quality collapses without it, we have learned the hidden reasoning was load-bearing and can pay for it knowingly. That test is running now.

Three of the obvious knobs are silent no-ops

This is the most immediately useful finding on the page, because the parameter most people would reach for first does nothing — and does nothing while returning HTTP 200. Identical prompt, max_tokens 1500:

Read the first two rows again. The default burned its entire budget on hidden reasoning and returned zero content — and "low" did exactly the same. It is not a mild reduction; it is the maximum setting, reached by a name that promises the opposite.

The cause is in the model's own chat template, which reads, in effect: use 'high' if the request said exactly 'high', otherwise use 'max'. Every value that is not the literal string high — including every word meaning "less" — is mapped to the most expensive setting there is. Only llama.cpp's special case for "none" escapes it.

"chat_template_kwargs": { "enable_thinking": false },
"cache_prompt": true

The general lesson generalises past this model: verify a performance parameter by measuring completion_tokens, never by checking that the call succeeded.

06Getting there

Two obstacles, neither of them the model.

The disk was full. 495 MB free on the machine's only volume, against a 342.7 GB download. Deleting finished training datasets and raw audio freed 559 GB; a dormant /swapfile2 — 100 GiB, dated April, absent from /proc/swaps and from /etc/fstab — gave back another 100. Free space now stands at 341 GB with the model on disk.

Nothing was installed. No llama-server, no vLLM or SGLang on PATH. The two that were present are not viable routes regardless: GLM-5.2 in bf16 is ~1.5 TB, more than twice the 640 GB of VRAM available, and neither stack's GGUF support covers a 754 B glm_moe_dsa MoE. llama.cpp publishes no prebuilt Linux CUDA binary, so it was built from source against the CUDA 12.8 toolkit already on the box.

One pleasant surprise in the architecture

GLM-5.2 uses multi-head latent attention, so llama.cpp caches only a 576-wide compressed latent with a single KV head. That is 92.8 KiB per token — 11.6 GiB at the full 128k context, and 2.9 GiB at 32k. On most large models the KV cache is what decides your GPU count. Here it is a rounding error: the weights decide, and the cache is nearly free.

06bDoes the quality survive? First results

Speed is worthless if the output degrades, and two things here could plausibly degrade it: a 3.64-bpw abliterated copy of the weights, and switching off the hidden reasoning that accounts for three quarters of generation. The flat trajectories column is the one that matters most, and it exists because of a specific warning. A probe asking for a character moving from guarded to trusting came back with five identical phases, all "guarded" — and that probe ran with thinking disabled. The grammar guarantees a value is in the enum; it guarantees nothing about it being the right member. Schema conformance is a syntax check, and a pipeline that only counts violations would score that failure as perfect. Nothing flat has appeared in the real scenes yet.

What is not yet shown
Structural scores are not literary judgement, and this arm is not finished.

These metrics count whether fields are filled and towers are built. They reward volume, and the local run is longer than the hosted one — so "better" rather than merely "more" rests on the completeness and depth-3 columns, not the word count. The thinking-high arm is still running, and the trajectories need reading by hand, not just counting.

07Where this stands

  1. It runs, on eight GPUs, at 15.7–21.7 tok/s decode and 255–396 tok/s prefill, producing correct structured output.
  2. Four GPUs is impossible at the only quantisation worth serving — 380.2 GiB measured against 314.9 GiB available. Five is the minimum, eight is comfortable.
  3. Eight GPUs do not make anything faster — not one request, and, it turns out, not many either. Layer-split is a pipeline, so at batch 1 seven cards wait; and concurrency was measured flat at ~27 tok/s from 1 to 8 streams. The eight cards buy the ability to run this model at this quantisation, and nothing else. That is still the whole reason to have them.
  4. Speculative decoding split both ways, and the split is the lesson. The n-gram drafter was a 25% regression at 21% acceptance; the model's own trained MTP head is a 45% gain at 79.6%. On a sparse MoE the technique is not mildly helpful or mildly unhelpful — it is strongly one or the other, decided by whether the drafter clears roughly 65%.
  5. For a single call, hosted remains faster. Local wins on data governance, on a pinned reproducible checkpoint, on cost at volume, and on not having a router between us and the weights — and after MTP and reasoning control, the gap is narrow enough that bulk generation belongs here.
  6. The lever was the boring one. Three quarters of generated tokens are hidden reasoning; turning it off is a 3.2×, and this pipeline has specific reason to survive without it because it already writes its reasoning out explicitly.
  7. And the biggest lever is not a decode question at all. Prompt caching cut a repeated 50 k prefix from 193.7 s to 0.064 s — a factor of 2,400, with one token reprocessed. Keeping the context prefix byte-stable across calls makes prefill effectively vanish. No amount of speculative decoding competes with not doing the work twice.
What this cost in wrong turns

Three confident predictions in this document were falsified by measurement, each within about an hour of being written:

  1. that low-entropy JSON would make speculative decoding shine — it made it 25% worse;
  2. that the MTP head needed a draft model the repository does not publish — it needed one flag;
  3. that a sparse MoE would batch well because concurrent sequences reuse expert reads — they route to different experts, and aggregate throughput never moved.

One and three were the same error twice: reasoning about a sparse MoE with intuitions built on dense models. Both times the argument sounded solid. The rule that survived — that widening the batch pays near-full cost on this architecture — was not derived, it was extracted from two failures and one success. Cheap experiments beat confident reasoning, and the correction cost minutes because the measurement was run first.
