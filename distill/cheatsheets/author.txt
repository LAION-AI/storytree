AUTHOR CHEAT SHEET — narrative reconstruction and distillation
==============================================================

HOW THIS FILE IS USED
---------------------
This file is SLICED, never injected whole. Each section carries a tag in
square brackets. A node-type prompt names the tags it wants; the loader
concatenates only those sections. Nothing else from this file reaches the
model on that call.

The reason is measured. EXP-001 (docs/experiments/EXP-001-model-notes-addendum.md)
added 4,561 characters of failure-derived instruction to a system prompt.
One clause improved its target metric on both items; one showed no effect;
one coincided with a REGRESSION on the only item where improvement was
possible. The live hypothesis is that instruction attention is itself a
budget, so added clauses displace clauses already there. Until EXP-002
settles it, we assume prompt fixes do not compose and we pay for every
clause we ship.

Blocks that are injected ALONGSIDE this file by the runtime, and are
therefore deliberately NOT repeated here:

  narrativeforge.craft.sheet()             the ~7,300-char Frey I & II +
                                           screen-craft sheet
  narrativeforge.craft.CRAFT_CHECKS        the compact per-node checklist
  narrativeforge.plotembedding.rubric_text() 52 genres x 24 dimensions,
                                           ROOT calls only
  narrativeforge.model_notes.addendum_for() the failure-derived model
                                           addendum, opt-in per EXP-001
  docs/10-prose-system-prompt.md           the prose ban list, prose calls only

If you find yourself restating something from those, delete it. Duplication
buys nothing and costs instruction budget.


[ALL] WHAT YOU ARE DOING
------------------------
You are recovering the hidden architecture of a screenplay that already
exists. You can see the script. You are not guessing what happens; you are
saying what the structure above it must have been for this to be the work
it is.

Three consequences:

1. The script is the authority. Where your reading and the script disagree,
   the script wins, and you say so.
2. Being right is not enough. A description that is accurate and generic is
   worth nothing. The transplant test governs everything you write: paste
   your node into a different work, change only the proper nouns, and if it
   still fits, it is wrong.
3. You are writing one node at a time on purpose. Do not produce a second
   deep structure to be helpful; it will be thin, and thinness in the second
   structure is the measured failure mode (docs/05 §1: four psychological
   analyses in one call produced four hollow shells, two of them with one
   field filled out of eleven, at unchanged total output length).


[ALL] THE FIVE THINGS THAT WILL BE SCORED HARDEST
-------------------------------------------------
Measured on this pipeline's own corpus, these are where nodes lose points:

  Independent-writer band (G), mean 2.33 across nine nodes — the weakest
  dimension by a wide margin. The question is not "is this defensible", it
  is "could a competent writer have produced this WITHOUT the inputs you
  were handed?" If a one-line prompt would have got the same node, the
  layer added nothing.

  Envelope discipline (T1), mean 1.67 — the metadata you are handed about a
  scene is a CONSTRAINT, not evidence. Location, roster, length. Both
  evaluated models put specimen lines in the mouths of characters not on
  the roster, 0 of 3 each.

  Change reality (V1), mean 1.00 for one model — declared state changes
  where before equals after. This is arithmetic, not craft. Check it.

  Spine causality (P1) and resistance reality (P2), both 2.50 — "and then,
  and then" spines, and declared opponents who never act.

  Deliberation honesty (T2) — alternatives that lose by a mile, flip
  conditions that say "if this were a different work", and confidence in
  the 90s on forecasts that are wrong in location, character and event.
  Before you write a confidence number, name the established fact that
  would have to be false for you to be wrong. If you cannot name one, your
  confidence is not a number, it is a mood.


[ALL] SPECIFICITY: WHAT COUNTS
------------------------------
Replace category words with things that could be false.

  "driven by her past"            -> the specific thing she does at 3am
  "precision, integrity, duty"    -> the rule she will not break and the
                                     one she already has
  "the conflict escalates"        -> who does what next, and why they must
  "atmospheric"                   -> what the light is doing, in this room
  "a mentor figure"               -> what he charges for the lesson

A claim that cannot be wrong is not a claim. Prefer the observable: a
behaviour, a measurement, a physical detail, a prohibition.


[ROOT] STORY ROOT
-----------------
Genre: never a single word. "Fantasy" and "action" name a shelf. Name the
tradition, the register, and at least one convention the work refuses. A
reader given only your genre string should be able to predict the shape of
the work, and should be surprised when the wrong films match it.

Audiences: possibly several distinct groups, each with a different reason
for being there. "Adults 18-49" is a demographic, not an audience.

Elevator pitch: a few sentences that pass two tests at once. Every clause
checks out against the script, AND a reader who has never heard of this
work understands the world, the protagonist's problem and the stakes, with
no unexplained proper nouns.

Writing style: the test is that you could describe a distinctive author's
style well enough for someone to write in it, without naming the author.
Pacing, dialogue register, atmosphere, introspective versus terse. Give at
least one falsifiable handle per axis: a thing the prose does, and a thing
it never does.

Dramatic structure: how many acts or chapters, where the turning points and
plot points sit, where the climax falls. Check your positions against the
work's measured scene indices, because the exposé's length budget is
derived from this and a wrong act boundary propagates.

Cast and important entities: characters, locations, groups, social and
abstract concepts, magic systems, technologies, conditions, objects. One to
three sentences each. Include everything whose removal would change the
plot; exclude everything else. Non-character entities are the ones that get
forgotten — check for them explicitly.

Plots: each a causal chain, one to three sentences. At least four in a
feature — main-character, character-growth, relationship, antagonist.
Longer works have more. Also consider a side-character plot, a plot
exploring one facet of human experience, and a historical or social theme.
Each plot must be separable: removing it must break something nameable.

Central topics and dilemmas: poverty, curiosity, greed, obligation, shame,
the things any reader relates to. State each as a dilemma with two
defensible sides, not as an abstract noun.

Identification value of the protagonist — BOTH halves, and this is a
requirement, not a preference:

  ADMIRE. A strength, virtue or competence that makes the audience want to
  be like them. Without it there is nothing to want.

  OPEN THE HEART. Human weaknesses the audience shares. Without them the
  character is perfect and no one identifies with perfection.

  A flawless character is boring. A character who is only flawed and
  incapable is a loser nobody wants to be. You need both, and they should
  be mechanically connected — the strength should cost something, or the
  flaw should be the price of the virtue.

  A villainous protagonist still needs the admirable half: competence,
  intelligence, style. Name which one.


[EXPOSE] EXPOSÉ
---------------
Length is derived, not chosen: about 300 words per act (a three-act feature
is about 900 words), about 200 words per chapter for a novel. The budget is
checked mechanically before anyone reads it.

Write for someone who has never heard of this story and will read it once.

  Explain the world — physics, social structures, governments, religions —
  ONLY where it matters. Every world fact you state must be consumed by an
  event later in the exposé. Every event that depends on a world fact must
  have that fact already stated. Nothing else belongs.

  Explain each important entity in context, at first use, assuming no prior
  knowledge and at the length its importance warrants.

  Give the chain of events in correct story order, honouring every plot.
  Honour them IMPLICITLY, through events. Never name a plot. "The
  relationship plot then develops" is a failure.

  Follow the ROOT's dramatic structure: its acts, its plot points, its
  turning points, in their declared positions.

  Explain in-world jargon — artifacts, powers, technologies, coined terms —
  in a clause at first use, enough to follow every later use.

  Say what makes the protagonist likeable and what opens the audience's
  heart, by showing a moment the script contains, not by asserting a trait.

  Weave in the human-experience topics through events where someone pays
  for them. No thesis sentences.

PROCESSING FLUENCY is an explicit criterion. The exposé must be readable on
the first pass without backtracking. Concretely: no sentence that has to be
re-read to be parsed; no pronoun whose referent resolves backwards; no term
used before it is introduced; no list longer than working memory. If you
have to hold something unresolved for more than a sentence, restructure.


[ENTITY] ENTITY PROFILES
------------------------
One entity per node. Protagonist first. For a character, the interview
covers all of:

  age; gender; looks (hair, face, build, attractiveness, skin colour,
  ethnicity); problem-solving strategy; coping strategies; social status;
  Big Five traits; goals; dreams; fears; vulnerabilities; weaknesses;
  strengths; internal conflict; relationships to every other character
  individually; a backstory of a few hundred words from childhood;
  intellectual capability; education; speech style; gait; habits; and
  off-cliché interests that make them round.

Add whatever else a novelist's character interview surfaces. The list is a
floor, not a ceiling.

Three rules that decide the score:

  t0 DISCIPLINE. This is the entity BEFORE anything happens. Everything
  forward-looking goes in `arc`. If the dossier knows how the story ends,
  every consumer downstream is handed the answer and the middle stops
  running on uncertainty.

  EVERY OTHER CHARACTER, INDIVIDUALLY. Not a blanket statement. For each:
  what this one wants from that one, what they believe that one thinks of
  them, and what they would never say to them. Asymmetry is the point —
  A's view of B is rarely B's view of A.

  INTERNAL CONFLICT IS COMPULSORY. Everyone has one, and it is usually two
  goals that cannot both be satisfied. Two goods, each with a named cost,
  each defensible. "Wants to do right but is afraid" is not a conflict,
  because you already know which side wins.

BACKSTORY FORMAT. Decompose it one self-contained claim per key, so it can
be patched later. A multi-sentence value under one key is a hole in the
state model. And make it causal: wound -> belief -> habit -> present
behaviour, at least twice. At least one belief the character holds should
be wrong in a way that will cost them.

OFF-CLICHÉ INTERESTS. At least two things they care about that the plot
never uses, that cut against their type, and that a reader would remember.
At least one should make a scene harder rather than easier.

For locations, objects, groups and concepts: same discipline, and say what
forces act on them and what could change their meaning.


[SCENE][EVENT] STATE, AND WHY NOTHING IS ARBITRARY
--------------------------------------------------
Every scene and event records, per involved entity: the state it enters
with, the changes it undergoes, and the state it exits with — mentally,
emotionally, socially, physically.

The hard requirement, at both levels: EVERY STATE CHANGE CONNECTS TO THE
OVERARCHING PLOTS AND THE DRAMATIC STRUCTURE. For each change, name

  (a) the plot and the step it discharges, and
  (b) the dramaturgical goal it serves at this position — setup for a named
      later payoff, reversal of a named earlier value, a cost that makes a
      named later choice harder.

"Raises tension" is a category word and fails. The test: could a reader
delete this change and say exactly which later thing stops working?

This requirement is what stops plot, character development, relationships,
themes and worldbuilding from drifting apart over a long work. It is not
bookkeeping. It is the mechanism.

An exit state is the entry state of the next unit. If they do not match,
one of them is wrong.


[SCENE][EVENT] MENTAL SIMULATION
--------------------------------
Per involved character, record: what they think, what they feel, what they
intend, what they perceive, what they express, and what they believe about
other minds. Displayed emotion versus felt emotion, with the leak specified
— the gap has to show somewhere. Internal conflict. Competing goals. Social
concerns. Higher-order theory of mind: what A thinks B thinks about A, and
the ERROR in that model, named and costed.

  EVENTS: beginning and end, and in between where it matters.
  SCENES: beginning, end, and EVERY BEAT that carries an important state
          change.
  Perception across all senses belongs at scene level.

Do one character per call. Do not batch. Measured: asking for four of these
in one call produced two hollow blocks with one field filled out of eleven,
while total output length stayed constant.

The purpose is a benchmark. With these hidden models reconstructed at
quality, a model can later be given only the script and asked: at what
point does Alice feel X while expressing Y? Your node either answers that
uniquely or it does not.


[SCENE][EVENT] CHARACTERS ARE PEOPLE, NOT PUPPETS
-------------------------------------------------
This is the failure mode the design exists to prevent, and it is scored
explicitly at scene and event level (dimension S3).

The failure: characters act only toward the obvious goal and react only to
the obvious threat. Everyone in the room is a function of the scene's
conflict. Delete the plot and the people cease to exist.

Real people in a scene are also connected to people who are not in it.
Things are happening elsewhere. They have a whole life, and this scene is
an interruption of it.

CHECKLIST. For each character present, at least three of these must be
true, specific, and at least one must measurably change a line, a timing,
an omission or an attention allocation inside the scene:

  ABSENT RELATIONSHIP    someone not in the room exerting pressure: a
                         person they owe a call, want to please, fear the
                         judgement of, are avoiding.
  ERRAND                 something they meant to do and have not. A thing
                         to collect, return, send, fix, pay.
  OBLIGATION             work or family, with a clock on it. A shift, a
                         pickup, a deadline, a visit.
  PRIVATE WORRY          not caused by this scene's conflict. Money, a
                         diagnosis, a child, a lie they told last week.
  BODILY NEED            hunger, thirst, cold, heat, exhaustion, pain, a
                         full bladder, a healing injury, a hangover.
  MICRO-DISTRACTION      a noise, an itch, a song stuck, a phone in a
                         pocket, a smell from next door.
  SOCIAL FEAR            what one specific named person will think.

RULES:
  - Specific to this character at this hour of this day. "Has a family" is
    not an item. "Has not called his sister since the funeral" is.
  - Inert items score 3 at best. The item must do something: shorten an
    answer, delay a decision, misdirect attention, make someone leave early.
  - It must not become the scene. One line's worth of pressure, not a
    subplot.
  - Vary it. If every character in every scene is hungry, you have replaced
    one tic with another.
  - Do not resolve them. An errand that gets done in-scene was a plot beat,
    not a life.


[SCENE] SCENE-SPECIFIC
----------------------
The envelope you are handed — location, time of day, who is on screen,
approximate length, dialogue ratio — is deterministic production metadata.
Treat it as a constraint you must satisfy, the way a writer is told the
scene plays in one room with two actors. It is not evidence about content.

  Restate the location and the roster before you decide anything.
  Nobody speaks who is not on the roster.
  Nobody's psychology is written who is not present.
  A 155-word beat is a beat, not a set piece.

The specimen exchange is the only falsifiable thing in the whole node.
"This character will open up here" cannot be checked. Six concrete lines
can be laid beside the real ones. Write them, then run the swap test:
give one character's line to another and say what breaks. If nothing
breaks, the voices are not separated yet.


[PROSE] WHEN YOU WRITE PROSE
----------------------------
The prose ban list in docs/10-prose-system-prompt.md is injected separately
and is not repeated here. Two things about it that are easy to get wrong:

  The claim that some models are better at subtext is NOT supported by the
  evidence and is contradicted by the only study that measured it. Do not
  build anything on it. The measured edge is lexical and structural
  discipline.

  Restraint is itself a tic register. Instructing toward restraint without
  capping restraint vocabulary swaps somatic tells for silences. The cap is
  in the ban list: at most ONE per scene of a character saying nothing, a
  character looking at something "for a long time", a described hand
  placement, or an explicit clock time — and vary which one.


[ALL] BEFORE YOU RETURN
-----------------------
  Arithmetic: counts, ages, dates, elapsed time, sums and shares recomputed,
  not estimated. Shares sum to 1.0.
  Every id resolves. Every reciprocal declaration exists.
  Every declared change has before != after.
  Every state change names a declared variable.
  Nothing you wrote would survive the transplant test.
  No field is a restatement of its own field name.
